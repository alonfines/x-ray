Abstract
Multiple hypothesis testing (MHT) frequently
arises in scientific inquiries, and concurrent
testing of multiple hypotheses inflates the risk
of Type-I errors or false positives, rendering
MHT corrections essential. This paper addresses MHT in the context of conformal prediction, a flexible framework for predictive
uncertainty quantification. Some conformal
applications give rise to simultaneous testing,
and positive dependencies among tests typically exist. We introduce max-rank, a novel
correction that exploits these dependencies
whilst efficiently controlling the family-wise
error rate. Inspired by existing permutationbased corrections, max-rank leverages rank order information to improve performance and
integrates readily with any conformal procedure. We establish its theoretical and empirical advantages over the common Bonferroni
correction and its compatibility with conformal prediction, highlighting the potential to
strengthen predictive uncertainty estimates.

BACKGROUND & RELATED
WORK

We first discuss our multiple testing setting and Type-I
error control, with a closer look at the Bonferroni correction. Then, we introduce the conformal prediction
setting and its connection to hypothesis testing.
Multiple testing in parallel and in series. A useful distinction can be made between statistical testing
in parallel and in series (Good, 1958). When testing in
parallel, multiple hypotheses are examined simultaneously. Frequently, conclusions are drawn on the basis
of the same underlying evidence such as a single common dataset (Vovk et al., 2022), making independence
assumptions harder to justify. This is our setting of
interest, and the one underlying conformal prediction
and any arising MHT issues (see § 6). In contrast,
testing in series suggests conclusions are drawn one
after another, and the outcome of a test may determine
whether and which subsequent hypotheses should be
explored. For example, a clinical drug trial with multiple phases may only initiate a follow-up if results are
satisfactory during the first phase (Bauer, 1991).
Family-wise error rate control. We can further distinguish among different types of Type-I error control
while testing: control in the strong sense via the FWER,
and in weaker forms via, e.g., the false discovery rate
(see § C.1). FWER control as the most stringent form
aims to limit the probability of committing at least
one Type-I error across all considered hypotheses. If
we test m hypotheses and denote by mFP the number
of falsely rejected null hypotheses (i.e., false positives),
then FWER control at significance level α ensures that
P(mFP ≥ 1) ≤ α.

(1)

This strict error control is appropriate in settings where
false discoveries may have strong implications, such as
in drug testing or clinical trials. Since conformal prediction is oftentimes motivated from a safety perspective
by its provided coverage guarantees and is applicable
to such settings (e.g., Lei and Candès (2021); Candès
et al. (2023); Yin et al. (2024)), FWER control is also
the appropriate notion here.

FWER control via the Bonferroni correction.
Common corrections with FWER control include Bonferroni (Sedgwick, 2012), Bonferroni-Holm (Aickin and
Gensler, 1996), or their Šidák-adjusted versions (Abdi
et al., 2007), which all presume independence. Arguably the simplest and most widely employed, the
classic Bonferroni correction adjusts each individual
significance level by the (fixed) number of tested hypotheses, i.e., αBonf = α/m. Then, FWER control
can be shown directly using an union bound argument
(see § C.2). However, the correction is well-known to
be overly conservative under positive dependency of
hypotheses (Vovk et al., 2022; Benjamini, 2010), and
requires specifying the number of tests m beforehand.
Multiple testing under dependency. Albeit MHT
issues have been addressed as early as Tukey (1953),
the research on valid and efficient corrections under
dependency remains remarkably active. Westfall and
Young (1993) suggested an early resampling-based procedure for capturing data dependencies, while recent
years have seen further work on arbitrary dependencies
(Causeur et al., 2009; Sun and Tony Cai, 2009; Leek
and Storey, 2008; Liu and Xie, 2019; Finner et al., 2017;
Gou and Tamhane, 2018; Chi et al., 2025) and p-value
merging (Wilson, 2019; Vovk et al., 2022; Chen et al.,
2023). Indeed, a remark by Chi et al. (2025) that “the
gap between theory and practice is mildly unsettling”
suggests these questions remain far from fully solved.
Multiple testing in conformal prediction. Conformal applications necessitating some form of multiple
testing correction are widespread, and include multistep time series and trajectory estimation (Stankeviciute et al., 2021; Sun and Yu, 2023; Xu et al., 2024;
Lindemann et al., 2023; Muthali et al., 2023); multitarget classification and regression (Cauchois et al.,
2021; Fisch et al., 2021; Messoudi et al., 2021, 2022;
Feldman et al., 2023), among them conformal object
detection (Andéol et al. (2023); Mukama et al. (2024),
see § 6.2); treatment analysis and screening (Lei and
Candès, 2021; Yin et al., 2024; Jin and Candès, 2023;
Fisch et al., 2022), and even as diverse as early-exiting
and diffusion models (Schuster et al., 2021; Teneggi
et al., 2023).
Throughout, employed MHT corrections frequently include Bonferroni (Stankeviciute et al., 2021; Lindemann
et al., 2023; Muthali et al., 2023; Andéol et al., 2023; Jin
and Candès, 2023), but can range from Copulas (Messoudi et al., 2021; Sun and Yu, 2023) to Simes (Fisch
et al., 2021) and other forms of sequential testing (Lei
and Candès, 2021; Yin et al., 2024; Cauchois et al., 2021;
Angelopoulos et al., 2021), showcasing a variety of considered solutions. Naturally, the application-specific
test design (in parallel vs. in series) largely governs the
suitability of employing particular corrections.

Conformal prediction. Conformal prediction (CP)
offers a distribution-free, post-hoc, and finite-sample approach to predictive uncertainty quantification (Vovk
et al., 2005; Shafer and Vovk, 2008). The obtained
uncertainty around a predictor’s output is expressed as
a prediction set, and provides an associated guarantee
on coverage (Eq. 2). The general algorithmic recipe for
split CP (Papadopoulos et al., 2007), its most popular
variant, is outlined in Algo. 1. Split CP requires a separate calibration dataset Dcal = {(Xi , Yi )}ni=1 ∼ PXY
for which dissimilarity values (so-called nonconformity
scores) between predicted and true outputs are computed. Given a new test sample (Xn+1 , Yn+1 ) ∼ PXY ,
a prediction set is then constructed by including any
predictions that align with the previously observed
scores on Dcal , as governed by a conformal quantile Q̂1−α ensuring a coverage rate of (1 − α) for
true values. The approach is distribution-free since
it does not impose any distributional assumptions.
Instead, a key requirement is the exchangeability of
Dcal ∪ {(Xn+1 , Yn+1 )}, a relaxed i.i.d. assumption
suggesting permutation invariance of samples. It is
post-hoc because it can be used with any ‘black-box’
pre-trained predictor without the need for training or
internal access. And it is finite-sample because the
obtainable coverage guarantee does not rely on asymptotics. Specifically, if CP’s conditions are met, a probabilistic statement on inclusion of Yn+1 in the prediction
set Ĉ(Xn+1 ) for a tolerated miscoverage rate α ∈ (0, 1)
is given as

Remark 2. The smallest possible quantile Q̂1−α which
maintains Type-I error control at level α is tight in
terms of coverage, i.e., it provides the smallest and
thus most efficient conformal prediction sets. We may
loosely interpret this as the matching permutation test
ensuring highest statistical power.

If the empirical nulls are uncorrelated and the tested
hypotheses can thus be considered independent, no

rank ordering, and max-rank will provide the optimal
quantile solution for each individual testing dimension.

3.2

Relation to Westfall & Young corrections

A strong connection exists between max-rank and the
max-T and min-P resampling-based MHT corrections of
Westfall and Young (1993), which offer similar FWER
control. The max-T method provides adjusted p-values
by comparing test statistics to the distribution of maximum statistics across hypotheses (Algo. 3), while the
min-P method adjusts p-values based on the minimum
observed p-values after resampling (Algo. 4). In both
cases, existing dependency structure between tests is
leveraged through repeated data resampling.

A. Timans, C.-N. Straehle, K. Sakmann, C. A. Naesseth, E. Nalisnick

Exploring these two corrections through the lens of our
conformal prediction setting, we find that max-rank
can be cast as a procedure that (i) naturally extends
max-T to the rank space, and (ii) describes a particular
instance of min-P arising from our problem setting. As
such, we argue that max-rank exhibits the advantages
of both methods by permitting its direct application to
obtained test statistics as in max-T, whilst maintaining
scale invariance as in min-P. We next relate to each
correction in more detail.
Relation to max-T. Our conformal setting directly provides empirical null distributions for every hypothesis
by use of the calibration set. The computed nonconformity scores on Dcal leverage available labels to produce
statistics known to satisfy the tested null H0 : Yn+1 = y,
and are thus interpretable as an analog to the required
“data resampling under the null” (Westfall and Young,
1993). Similarly, CP’s exchangeability condition aligns
with the exchangeability underlying the resampling
step. It follows that the adjusted p-values in Algo. 3
match the conformal p-values in Eq. 3 simply by replacing the score set S with a maximum score set Smax ,
i.e., replacing P̂n+1 (y; S) with P̂n+1 (y; Smax )1 . Instead
of adjusting the p-values as in Algo. 3 we may also
directly target an adjustment of the conformal quantile
(Eq. 4), which translates to computing Q̂(1 − α; Smax )
instead of Q̂(1−α; S). Comparing this to max-rank, we
notice that the difference lies in its direct application to
the maximum statistics, rather than translating them
into rank space first (i.e., using Q̂(1 − α; rmax )). This
means that max-T is sensitive to scale and requires test
statistics across hypotheses to be directly comparable.
Interestingly, such a “maximum of scores” approach has
been intuitively employed by researchers in conformal
prediction, e.g., Andéol et al. (2023); Schuster et al.
(2021); Fisch et al. (2022), but never been recognized
as an application case of Westfall and Young (1993).
Relation to min-P. By the same resampling interpretation as above, unadjusted p-values for each hypothesis
in Algo. 4 can be computed via conformal p-values
(Eq. 3). Using the rank definition in Eq. 5, a given
p-value P̂n+1 (y; S) can be expressed in terms of score
ranking as ((n + 1) − rank(sn+1 ; S))/(n + 1)2 . Thus,
computing the minimum p-values in min-P can be interpreted as the maximum over inverted and normalized
ranks. Via Eq. 4 we again translate adjusted p-values
to a quantile adjustment using Q̂(α; pmin ). Note that
this quantile is now taken over p-values and only permits testing in p-value space. In contrast, max-rank
keeps the simplicity of operating directly on statistics,
facilitating the interpretation of testing decisions.

SIMULATION STUDY

We empirically demonstrate our two results in Prop. 1
and Prop. 2 via a simulation study. We simulate X
as a multivariate random variable whose m components may be jointly positively correlated. Specifically,
we simulate X ∼ N (µm , Σm×m ) with µm = 0m and
Σm×m = ρ · 1m×m + (1 − ρ) · Im×m . Thus, covariance
entries on the off-diagonals are determined by the correlation strength ρ ∈ [0, 1], while the diagonal denotes
perfect correlation. The desired (global) significance
level for FWER control is fixed at α = 0.05, and we
vary the correlation strength ρ, number of tests m, and
number of statistics n for every component Xk of X.
Global FWER control. We compare the adjusted
significance levels produced by both max-rank and
Bonferroni across varying correlation strengths and for
m ∈ {1, 3, 5, 10, 20} in Fig. 1. We observe two key drawbacks for Bonferroni. Firstly, the correction becomes

increasingly conservative as correlation between tests
increases, with a FWER control level tending towards
zero. Secondly, the rate accelerates as the number of
tests increases. In contrast, max-rank suffers from neither of these behaviours and tightly maintains FWER
control at target level α, ensuring consistent results
independently of correlation strength and test dimensionality (Prop. 1).
Individual Type-I error control. We next compare
the adjusted significance levels obtained by max-rank
and Bonferroni for individual testing dimensions k ∈
[m] and for fixed m = 5 against varying correlation
strengths in Fig. 2. Bonferroni limits individual Type-I
error levels to α̂Bonf = α/m = 0.01 in order to achieve
FWER control, forcing overly conservative behaviour
in each test. In contrast, max-rank is able to exploit
any positive dependency between tests, resulting in
reduced requirements on individual Type-I error levels
as the correlation increases (Prop. 2). This, of course,
strongly benefits the chances of a significant result in
each dimension. Meanwhile, we observe in Fig. 1 for
m = 5 that the FWER remains controlled.
FWER control and sample size (§ D.1). Since
max-rank relates to Westfall and Young (1993) and is
thus interpretable as a resampling-based correction, we
anticipate the sample size n (equivalent to the number

of resamples) to affect FWER control, in particular
when running many tests in parallel. Thus, we verify in Fig. 4 adjusted significance levels for max-rank

correspondence between FWER control and the magnitudes of m and n is apparent: as we run more tests

A key observation is that due to their joint dependence
on common calibration data Dcal , obtained conformal
p-values in any single dimension are bound to exhibit
some degree of positive dependency, a property known
as positive regression dependent on a subset (PRDS)
(Benjamini and Yekutieli, 2001; Bates et al., 2022; Chi
et al., 2025). This highlights that any MHT correction
relying on an independence assumption (such as Bonferroni) is bound to be overly conservative a priori, and
our empirical results point towards a similar positive
dependency across testing dimensions for CP settings.
6.1

Using max-rank for conformal prediction

Since we have closely connected max-rank’s formulation to conformal prediction, one can rightly assume
that it integrates well with any conformal procedure
following Algo. 1. To rigorously assert this, we verify
that max-rank meets two key requirements of conformal prediction: (i) the exchangeability condition on the
underlying data is preserved, and (ii) Eq. 7 is corrected
in such a way that the conformal coverage guarantee
from Eq. 2 is upheld.
We demonstrate in § A.4 that max-rank satisfies both
conditions by analysing its two key operations, namely
(i) operating in the rank space of nonconformity scores,
and (ii) applying a max-operation on these ranks
(or more generally, any set of exchangeable random
variables). While the requirement on valid coverage
technically directly follows from max-rank’s FWERcontrolling property (Prop. 1), an additional proof is
provided via rank coverage. We summarize these insights in the following proposition:
Proposition 3. The max-rank procedure (Algo. 2)
provides a multiple testing correction for conformal
prediction that preserves exchangeability and ensures
valid coverage (Eq. 2).

7

DISCUSSION

We introduce max-rank, an efficient MHT correction
for testing under positive dependency which adapts
Westfall and Young (1993)’s corrections to conformal
prediction. While we provide strong theoretical and
empirical results, our method’s benefits are mostly
restricted to (i) data dependencies that become apparent through rank ordering, (ii) settings of positive
dependency, since we do not benefit from rank information under negative dependency and thus do not improve upon Bonferroni, and (iii) its permutation-based,
data-driven nature necessitates a sufficient ratio of test
statistics n to test dimensions m for empirical FWER
control. Future work includes establishing stronger theoretical results, such as on convergence rates to target
level α, the dependence between quantities m, n, and
α, and its relationship to the empirical copula. Naturally, we may also experimentally extend to further
applications such as multi-step time series (Sun and
Yu, 2023). We hope that max-rank contributes to the
ongoing research on multiple testing under dependency,
all the while addressing practical needs in predictive
settings using conformal prediction.

A.4

Using max-rank for conformal prediction

Two key conditions are imposed on any multiple testing correction that integrates with conformal prediction: (i)
the exchangeability condition on the underlying data is preserved, and (ii) Eq. 7 is corrected in such a way that
the conformal coverage guarantee is upheld. We next show that max-rank satisfies both conditions by looking
at its two key operations, namely (i) operating in the rank space of nonconformity scores, and (ii) applying a
max-operation to these ranks. We first address the exchangeability requirement, followed by validity.
Preserving exchangeability. Our arguments rely on work by Kuchibhotla (2020), which establishes connections
between exchangeability and rank-based hypothesis testing. Recall our rank definition in Eq. 5. For operating in
the rank space of nonconformity scores, we make use of the following theorem:
Theorem 1 (Distribution of ranks (Kuchibhotla, 2020), Thm. 2). For exchangeable random variables X1 , . . . , Xn
we have that

exchangeable itself, since the ranks are uniformly distributed and do not depend on the distribution of scores. We

ADDITIONAL BACKGROUND

We provide additional details on FDR control (§ C.1) and the Bonferroni correction (§ C.2).
C.1

FDR control

In contrast to the stronger notion of FWER control which bounds the probability of committing at least one
Type-I error, controlling the false discovery rate (FDR) targets a limit on the number of false discoveries in
expectation across hypothesis tests. This weaker notion of Type-I error control comes at the benefit of a larger
fraction of significant discoveries (Shaffer, 1995). Denote the number of false positives mFP as before, and the
fraction of correct discoveries (i.e., true positives) as mTP . Then, FDR control at significance level α ensures that
Em (mFP /(mFP + mTP )) ≤ α,

(18)

where the expectation is taken over the m hypotheses. FDR control is appropriate in settings in which a limited
proportion of false positives can be tolerated in exchange for a greater chance at significant results, such as
in genomics studies (Han et al., 2009). Commonly known methods for FDR control include the BenjaminiHochberg (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995), Benjamini-Yekutieli (Benjamini and Yekutieli, 2001) and Simes
corrections (Simes, 1986)5 . In contrast to classical corrections with FWER control which assume independence,
FDR-controlling procedures are generally more robust to dependencies between tests. Beyond well-known FWER
and FDR control, extensions such as k-FWER (Romano and Wolf, 2007; Janson and Su, 2016) – which bounds
the probability of committing more than k Type-I errors (recovering FWER for k = 1) – and false discovery
exceedance (Genovese and Wasserman, 2004) – which controls tail probabilities of the false discovery proportion
(recovering FDR for the expectation) – have also been explored.
C.2

We demonstrate how the Bonferroni correction ensures FWER control using a simple union bound argument.

nature, whereas we do not specify here how the Bonferroni p-values are computed. Let us label the fraction of
hypotheses that should be correctly marked as non-significant (i.e., true negatives) as mTN . The null is rejected
when the respective p-value reaches below significance level, i.e., when we have Pk ≤ αBonf for some test k.

D.1

Simulation study: FWER control and sample size

Since max-rank connects to Westfall and Young (1993)’s corrections as a resampling-based method, we anticipate
that its data-driven nature requires an adequate ratio of tests counts m to sample sizes n per test (equivalent to
the number of resamples or permutations) in order to empirically provide FWER control. Thus, we verify in Fig. 4
adjusted significance levels for max-rank and Bonferroni across varying combinations of correlation ρ, test counts
m, and samples n. Specifically, we consider ρ ∈ {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0}, m ∈ [1, 1000], and n ∈ {100, 500, 1000}. In
particular, this also includes high-dimensional settings where m > n.
Our results confirm the anticipated dependencies. Specifically, empirical FWER control is not guaranteed when
the ratio of samples to test dimensions is too low, including the settings where m > n. These violations affect
both max-rank and Bonferroni, albeit our correction tends to violate sooner since it operates closer to target level.
However, FWER control rates improve substantially as both sample size and correlation increases. Intuitively,
higher correlation effectively reduces the amount of independent rank order information across tests that needs to
be accounted for. Overall, we observe that empirical FWER control is approximately maintained when n ≥ 100 · m
in the least favorable setting (ρ = 0). However, there are cases where this rate can be exceeded (e.g., n = 100 and
m = 5 holds) or fall short (e.g., n = 1000 and m = 10 undercorrects slightly). Finally, throughout all considered
combinations Bonferroni is always more conservative than max-rank (in line with Prop. 2), and its disadvantage
to max-rank increases substantially as the correlation goes up.
The observed sample requirements seem reasonable given that a reliable empirical null distribution must be
established for each test dimension, and are somewhat in the range of calibration set sizes commonly used in

Max-Rank: Efficient Multiple Testing for Conformal Prediction

conformal prediction. For instance, Angelopoulos et al. (2023) recommend at least 1000 calibration samples for a
robust conformal procedure even in a single dimension, while Messoudi et al. (2021) (see Table 2 in their paper)
consider varying sample requirements for multi-target regression such as (n, m) ∈ {(2100, 3), (24160, 4), (910, 8)}.
The impact of overly small sample sizes on results is evident both in their work (e.g., Fig. 3 in their paper) and
ours (Table 1, for the scpf dataset).
In general, the observed interactions between quantities seem consistent and suggest a meaningful relationship
that warrants future theoretical investigation, as mentioned in § 7. It would be desirable to quantify the expected
FWER control achievable for a given significance level α, sample size n and number of tests m in analytical form.

D.4

Conformal multi-target regression

We provide a few more details on our experimental design, which follows closely Messoudi et al. (2021). We select
a subset of three interesting datasets with varying numbers of regression targets and sample size lengths7 : scpf
(website traffic data, m = 3, 1137 samples), rf1 (river flow data, 9125 samples, m = 8), and scm1d (product
pricing data, 9803 samples, m = 16). We use the data pre-processing scheme of Messoudi et al. (2021)8 and
similarly train a multi-target random forest regressor using sci-kit learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011) with minimal
hyperparameter tuning. Each dataset is randomly split into 60% training, 20% calibration, and 20% test data,
and the model training and conformal procedure is repeated and results averaged across 100 random trials for
robust evaluation.
D.5

Conformal object detection

Object detection tasks consist of a dual problem wherein both an object’s location, as parametrized via it’s
four bounding box coordinates (thus m = 4), and the object’s class label need to be predicted. We focus on
applying conformal prediction to the bounding box prediction task, which forms a regression problem in the
(approximately) continuous pixel space of input images. Our experimental design strongly relates to Mukama
et al. (2024) but builds upon our own developed conformal methodology for object detection (Timans et al., 2024).
Instead of considering only objects of a single class ‘car’, we instead apply conformal prediction separately to a
variety of classes. This is easily achieved by filtering the labelled calibration data by class label, and computing
class-specific conformal quantiles via Algo. 1 used to build prediction sets for each test sample based on its
(unconformalized) class label. Thus, our results in Fig. 3 averaged across multiple classes (and trials) provide a
more robust performance evaluation of each multiple testing correction.
In particular, we employ the BDD100k dataset (Yu et al., 2020) in our experiment, and thus consider the classes
{person, bicycle, car, motorcycle, bus, truck, traffic light, stop sign} with varying object counts9 . We employ a
pre-trained object detection model using detectron2 (Wu et al., 2019) based on a Faster R-CNN architecture
(model type X101-FPN10 ), and thus do not require training on BDD100k. Instead we split all available labelled
data into calibration (70%) and test sets (30%), and the conformal procedure is repeated and results are collected
across 100 random trials. We additionally report per-class results in Table 3 for completeness, affirming that
drawn conclusions on max-rank’s strong performance hold also individually by class. We also display in Fig. 5 a
visual example of how the constructed prediction intervals around objects look like. We observe that interval
regions (shaded in orange) seem reasonably tight and tend to center around the ground truth bounding boxes (in
blue). The max-rank correction ensures that the constructed intervals provide a valid coverage guarantee over
the true object locations according to Eq. 2.
