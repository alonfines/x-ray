RESEARCH PROPOSAL
Title: Trustworthy GenAI for Radiology Reports via Conformal Validation of Statements
Project coordinator: Tammy Riklin Raviv

1.

Abstract

Generative AI is rapidly changing the technical landscape of medical image interpretation. In radiology, large
vision-language models can already produce fluent, plausible reports from imaging studies. Yet fluency is not
clinical validity. A generated report may contain statements that are unsupported by the image, omit clinically
important observations, or express certainty when the visual evidence is ambiguous. Such errors may hinder their
incorporation into routine clinical practice, as they raise unresolved concerns regarding reliability, uncertainty,
and safety.
This proposal develops a framework for evidence-constrained generative radiology. It moves beyond standard
report-generation evaluation, which often relies on text similarity, label extraction, or uncalibrated factuality
scoring. Instead, it probes the statistical validity of image-conditioned clinical statements implicitly asserted by the
text, addressing their dependencies while preserving clinically relevant modifiers. Structured conformal predictions
will be used to obtain calibrated prediction for sets of related radiology statements with formal guarantees. These
guarantees will then be translated into explicit generative behaviors, namely assertion, qualification, or abstention,
thereby aligning model behavior with clinical intent. The research will investigate the mathematical properties of
conformalized statements, the emergence of epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty through conformal set behavior,
and the limits of safe generation under imbalanced or rare findings, partial observability and domain shift. The
outcome is a theoretically grounded and practically applicable framework for medical GenAI systems in which
free-text outputs must be constrained by evidence.
2.

Scientific background and state of the art

Radiology GenAI and the safety gap. Radiology reports are evidential clinical documents: they communicate
what is justified by imaging data. Thus, the central risk in report generation is not stylistic failure, but clinically
consequential statements that are unsupported, insufficiently qualified, or omitted despite being relevant.
Current evaluation methods for radiology report generation do not fully address this safety problem. Lexical
metrics such as BLEU or ROUGE are poorly aligned with clinical correctness. Label-based scores provide useful
summaries but collapse rich report language into a limited set of findings. Entity- and relation-based approaches,
such as RadGraph, move closer to clinical structure by extracting findings, anatomical locations, and relations
(Jain et al., 2021). Newer evaluation frameworks such as GREEN attempt to identify clinically significant errors in
generated reports (Ostmeier et al., 2024). Studies of report-generation progress and metrics have also shown that
clinically meaningful metrics such as RadGraph F1 and RadCliQ provide more useful evaluation than text overlap
alone (F. Yu et al., 2023). These developments demonstrate that the field is moving away from surface-level text
similarity. However, they still largely evaluate generated reports after the fact, without providing a principled
statistical mechanism that determines what a model may safely assert, qualify, or withhold.
From report generation to evidence-constrained communication. Recent radiology vision-language models

and report-generation systems, including large multimodal and dialog-oriented models, demonstrate the rapid
progress of the field (Pellegrini et al., 2023). Methods for improving factual completeness and consistency
have emphasized that generated reports must be evaluated not only for incorrect statements but also for missing
clinically important information (Miura et al., 2021). The proposed project builds on this direction but changes
the target of analysis: from the generated report as a whole to the set of dependent image-conditioned clinical
statements it communicates.
Structured report information and report-derived labels. Report-derived labels and information extraction are
essential for large-scale medical AI. CheXpert introduced uncertainty-aware labels for chest radiographs (Irvin
et al., 2019), while CheXbert showed that BERT-based labelers can improve automatic radiology report labeling
using expert annotations and rule-based supervision (Smit et al., 2020). MIMIC-CXR and MIMIC-CXR-JPG
provide large public resources of chest radiographs, free-text reports, and structured labels that have enabled
much of the modern literature in chest imaging AI (Johnson, Pollard, Berkowitz, et al., 2019; Johnson, Pollard,
Greenbaum, et al., 2019). These datasets and labelers are important empirical resources, but the proposal does
not reduce the scientific contribution to a specific dataset or label list. They will be used to instantiate and test a
general framework for evidence-calibrated statements.
Conformal prediction and medical uncertainty. Uncertainty quantification is central to medical AI. A medical
GenAI system should not only produce an answer; it should know when the evidence is insufficient. Conformal
prediction provides a distribution-free framework for constructing prediction sets with finite-sample coverage
guarantees under exchangeability (Vovk et al., 2005; Shafer and Vovk, 2008; Angelopoulos* and Bates, 2023).
In its standard form, conformal prediction produces a set that contains the true output with probability at least
1 − α. This is attractive for medicine because uncertainty can be communicated explicitly rather than hidden
behind a single prediction. Related work has developed conformalized quantile regression (Romano et al., 2019),
risk-controlling prediction sets (Bates et al., 2021), conformal risk control (Angelopoulos et al., 2024), and
multi-label prediction sets with limited false positives (Fisch* et al., 2022). These methods provide important
statistical tools, but they do not directly define the clinical unit of validation for free-text medical generation.
Conditional calibration, class imbalance, and rare statements. Marginal coverage over all statements may
be misleading in radiology. Statement distributions are highly imbalanced: common normal or chronic findings
dominate the data, while rare but clinically important findings may have limited calibration support. A system can
appear well calibrated overall while being unreliable for rare findings, uncertain statements, specific anatomical
regions, or high-risk clinical concepts. Class-conditional and clustered conformal prediction directly address
the difficulty of obtaining stronger coverage guarantees when classes are numerous or imbalanced (Ding et al.,
2023). Work on conditional histograms, label-noise robustness, missing values, and corrupted labels further
demonstrates that calibration must be adapted to the structure and imperfections of real data (Sesia and Romano,
2021; Einbinder et al., 2022; Zaffran et al., 2023; Feldman* et al., 2025). This project aim to bring these ideas to
image-conditioned clinical statements, where the conditioning variables are not only labels but also semantic,
clinical, and representation-based neighborhoods.
Medical imaging uncertainty beyond scalar prediction. Medical imaging introduces additional structure that
is often absent from standard classification problems. Predictions may be spatial, multi-label, correlated across
anatomy, affected by acquisition protocol, and tied to clinical language rather than to a single endpoint. Recent
work on uncertainty quantification for imaging has therefore emphasized the need to move beyond independent

pixel-wise or label-wise confidence scores toward structured uncertainty objects, including spatially coherent
uncertainty, conformal masks, and clinically interpretable uncertainty displays (Belhasin et al., 2024; Kutiel*
et al., 2023). These ideas are highly relevant here: the proposed framework treats uncertainty as a property of
image-conditioned clinical communication. The object to be calibrated is not only an image classifier output, but
the evidence relationship between an image and a statement.
The remaining gap. The key scientific gap is not simply classifier calibration, but the calibration of the
relationship between an image and a dependent set of image-conditioned clinical statements. Report statements
may encode presence, absence, uncertainty, severity, laterality, chronicity, temporal change, or omission, and these
states may imply, qualify, contradict, or constrain one another. The proposed project reframes report generation as
evidence-constrained clinical communication, centered on a validation unit that preserves clinical meaning while
enabling structured, conditional, and multidimensional calibration.
3.

Research objectives and specific aims

The overall objective is to develop a framework for evidence-constrained generative radiology, in which generated
reports are validated as sets of image-conditioned clinical statements rather than as free text or isolated labels.
The central hypothesis is that report generation becomes safer and more clinically useful when each statement is
assigned a calibrated evidence status relative to the image, and report language is constrained accordingly.
The main methodological contribution is structured conformal validation of dependent clinical statements. The
project will define the validation unit, represent dependencies among statements, construct image-conditioned
nonconformity scores, calibrate admissible evidence assignments, and translate the calibrated sets into report-level
behaviors such as assertion, qualification, abstention, or flagging for review.
Aim 1: Define image-conditioned clinical statements as validation units. We will formalize each report
statement as a structured object encoding a clinical concept, assertion state, and modifiers such as location,
laterality, severity, temporal change, or uncertainty. This aim establishes the basic unit on which statistical
validation is performed.
Aim 2: Model dependencies among statements and define structured nonconformity. We will represent
relations among normalized statements using a corpus-level dependency graph and combine image-statement
evidence with statement-compatibility terms. The resulting structured nonconformity score will measure whether
a joint assignment of evidence states is supported by both the image and the learned statement dependencies.
Aim 3: Develop structured conformal calibration for dependent statements. We will apply conformal
calibration to structured nonconformity scores to obtain sets of admissible joint evidence assignments. Unlike
standard conformal prediction for isolated labels, the calibrated object will describe which combinations of
statement evidence states are statistically supported.
Aim 4: Address imbalance, rare findings, and multidimensional clinical risk. We will evaluate calibration
across clinically meaningful strata, including evidence state, anatomical region, statement type, and findingfrequency group. Rare findings will be grouped or handled conservatively when calibration data are insufficient.
We will also use scalar clinically weighted losses, where appropriate, to study communication risks such as
unsupported assertion, contradiction, omission, overconfidence, and inappropriate uncertainty.
Aim 5: Extend validation from stated content to omissions and report behavior. We will probe candidate

statements absent from the report to detect possible omissions of clinically important findings. Calibrated evidence
will then be translated into report-level behavior: assert when supported, qualify when ambiguous, abstain or flag
when unsupported, and indicate possible omissions when absent statements are image-supported.
4.

Detailed description of the proposed research

4.1 Validation unit: image-conditioned clinical statements
Let X ∈ X denote the observed radiological imaging study, such as a radiograph, CT volume, or MRI scan,
and let T denote a generated or human-written report. The framework does not assume access to the internal
report generator. It treats the report as a set of structured clinical statements S(T ) = s1 , . . . , sm . Each statement
sj = (cj , aj , µj ) ∈ S encodes a clinical concept cj , an assertion state aj , and optional clinically relevant modifiers
µj , such as location, laterality, severity, temporal change, or uncertainty language.
For each statement, the validation task is to determine its evidence status with respect to the image, Y (X, sj ) ∈ Y,
where, in the simplest setting, Y = {supported, contradicted, underdetermined}. Thus, the basic unit of
validation is not a word, sentence, or dataset label alone, but an image-conditioned clinical statement.

4.2 Representing dependencies among statements
To account for dependencies among clinical statements, we define a corpus-level graph over normalized statements
extracted from radiology reports. Graph-based representations have been used for pathology co-occurrence and
structured radiology information Chen et al., 2020; Li et al., 2019; Jain et al., 2021. Here, the graph serves as a
dependency prior within a structured conformal validation framework, rather than as a standalone graph-learning
model.
Let GD = (VD , ED ) denote a corpus-level dependency graph constructed from a corpus D of D reports. The nodes
VD = {V1 , . . . , VK } are normalized clinical statements, and ED = (eij ) ∈ [−1, 1]K×K is a weighted adjacency
matrix encoding pairwise relations among statements. Each node Vk is associated with a report-occurrence vector
(vk,1 , . . . , vk,D ),

vk,d ∈ {−1, 0, 1}, where vk,d = 1 indicates assertion in report d, vk,d = −1 indicates

explicit absence or negation, and vk,d = 0 indicates non-mention or unresolved status.
The edge weight eij represents the relation between statements Vi and Vj . Positive values indicate compatibility or
co-occurrence, negative values indicate contradiction or clinical inconsistency, and values near zero indicate weak
or no dependency. Edge weights may be estimated from report-level co-occurrence, negation and uncertainty
patterns, and RadGraph-style entity and relation annotations Jain et al., 2021.
For an individual report T , the relevant dependency structure is the subgraph induced by the statements in
S(T ), possibly augmented by candidate high-priority statements for omission detection. These dependencies are
incorporated into the structured nonconformity score through compatibility terms between evidence states. Thus,
the graph provides a practical representation of statement dependencies, while the main contribution is conformal
calibration of dependent image-conditioned statements.

4.4 Structured conformal calibration
Given a calibration set of image-report pairs with reference evidence states, conformal calibration will be applied
to the structured nonconformity scores. For a target level 1 − α, the calibrated prediction object is a set of
admissible joint evidence assignments,
Γα (X, T ) = YT : Astr (X, T, YT ) ≤ qα ,
where qα is the calibration quantile. This object provides calibrated evidence for the report as a dependent set of
statements, rather than independent confidence scores for isolated findings.
The resulting evidence sets will be translated into report behavior. A uniquely supported statement may be asserted.
A statement whose evidence set includes underdetermination should be qualified or deferred. A contradicted
statement should be withheld or flagged. Broad or unstable evidence sets indicate that the report should be
reviewed rather than presented as definitive.

4.6 Candidate-statement probing for omissions
Validating only statements that appear in a report cannot detect clinically important omissions. We will therefore
construct a candidate set Q(X, T ) of statements that are absent from the report but potentially relevant to the
image. Candidates may be obtained from the clinical statement vocabulary, high image-evidence scores, retrieval
in the learned representation space, or clinician-defined high-priority findings.
For each candidate q ∈ Q(X, T ), we evaluate an augmented statement set
S + (T, q) = S(T ) ∪ {q}.
The conformal machinery is then applied to the possible evidence states of q, yielding a calibrated set
Γqα (X, T ) = {yq : ∃YT such that Astr (X, T, S + (T, q), (YT , yq )) ≤ qα }.
If Γqα (X, T ) strongly supports a present or clinically reportable state for a statement absent from the report, the
system flags a possible omission. If the evidence is ambiguous, the omission is qualified rather than asserted. If
the candidate is not supported, no omission is flagged. These omission indicators contribute to the report-level
risk component Romit defined above. Together, Sections 4.1–4.6 define the proposed validation pipeline, from
structured statement extraction and dependency representation to calibrated evidence assignments and omission
probing. The next sections describe the datasets and preliminary experiments used to instantiate and evaluate this
framework.

4.7 Datasets and clinical statement spaces
The project will use two complementary public radiology domains. For chest radiography, we will use MIMICCXR and MIMIC-CXR-JPG Johnson, Pollard, Berkowitz, et al., 2019; Johnson, Pollard, Greenbaum, et al.,
2019, which provide chest radiographs paired with free-text reports and structured report-derived labels. This
domain supports experiments on common findings such as atelectasis, cardiomegaly, consolidation, edema, pleural
effusion, pneumonia, and pneumothorax.
For MRI, we will use MR-RATE Forithmus, 2026, which provides brain and spine MRI studies paired with
radiology reports and pathology labels. This domain will test the framework under longer reports, heterogeneous
anatomy, multi-sequence imaging, and more complex statement structures.
In both domains, the clinical statement space will be constructed from normalized report findings and modifiers,
including anatomical location, laterality, severity, uncertainty, and negation. Frequent findings will support stratumspecific calibration, while rare but clinically important findings will be grouped or evaluated conservatively without
claiming label-specific validity.

4.8 Evaluation, clinical input, and contingency plan
Evaluation will include coverage, conformal set size, abstention rate, subgroup behavior, and communication-risk
components: unsupported assertion, contradiction, omission, overconfident wording, and inappropriate uncertainty.
Ablations will compare independent finding-level calibration, structured calibration without dependency terms,

Page 7 of 12

and the full dependency-aware framework.
Clinical input will be obtained through collaboration with radiology experts at Soroka Medical Center. This
collaboration will support definition of clinically meaningful statement vocabularies, prioritization of high-risk
omissions, interpretation of uncertainty language, and qualitative review of selected failure cases. The core
experiments will rely on public datasets; any use of local clinical data will be pursued only subject to the required
institutional approvals.
If statement extraction is noisy, we will restrict the vocabulary to high-confidence normalized statements. If
dependency estimation is unstable, we will use simpler co-occurrence statistics, clinical rules, or expert-defined
relations. If rare findings lack sufficient calibration samples, they will be grouped by clinical or anatomical
similarity, or evaluated using global calibration without label-specific validity claims. If full report-generation
integration is not feasible, the framework will be evaluated as a post-hoc validator of generated or reference
reports.

As a preliminary feasibility study, we evaluated a simplified finding-level conformal prediction framework on
23,462 chest radiographs from 14,876 CheXpert patients across five pathologies. Unlike the proposed framework,
this experiment uses independent binary finding labels and does not yet model report text, modifiers, omissions, or

Page 8 of 12

dependencies among structured clinical statements. Ground-truth labels extracted from radiology reports indicate
whether each finding is present, absent, or uncertain. To prevent patient-level leakage, the data were partitioned
into disjoint training (67%), validation (10%), calibration (10%), and test (13%) sets, with label distributions
preserved across splits.
The baseline model was a DenseNet121 multi-label classifier (Huang et al., 2017), trained with binary crossentropy loss. To define a controlled binary setting, uncertain cases were excluded from training and calibration,
which used only definite positive and definite negative labels. We then applied class-conditional conformal
prediction, computing separate nonconformity thresholds for present and absent labels to reduce the effect of
class imbalance, with target coverage 1 − α = 0.9.
At test time, uncertain cases were retained to probe whether expert uncertainty corresponds to conformal ambiguity.
Singleton prediction sets, {Present} or {Absent}, were treated as decisive predictions, while the non-singleton
set {Present, Absent} was treated as abstention. As shown in Table 1, conformal prediction achieved close
to nominal coverage for definite positive and negative labels. The results also show that expert uncertainty
and conformal abstention are related but not equivalent: uncertainty-to-abstention varies substantially across
pathologies. This supports the need for the proposed statement-level framework, where uncertainty, omission,
modifiers, and dependencies are modeled explicitly rather than treated as a third class.

Significance, innovation and potential benefits of the proposed research

This project reframes the safety problem of generative medical AI from report similarity to evidence-constrained
communication: what is a model clinically justified in saying, given the image?
The main innovation is a structured validation unit: the image-conditioned clinical statement. Unlike labels or
whole-report scores, statements preserve clinically relevant meaning, including presence, absence, uncertainty,
modifiers, contradictions, and omissions. The second innovation is structured conformal validation of dependent statements. Rather than calibrating isolated findings, the framework calibrates admissible joint evidence
assignments, accounting for image support and statement dependencies. The third innovation is the translation of
calibrated evidence into report behavior: assertion, qualification, abstention, or omission flagging.
The expected benefit is a safety layer for medical GenAI systems that goes beyond fluency and factuality scoring.
It can support clinician-supervised drafting, pre-deployment auditing, model comparison, and monitoring of
unsupported assertions, uncertainty mismatch, contradictions, and omissions. More broadly, the project provides
a framework for free-text medical AI outputs that must remain accountable to observed evidence.
6.

Applicability

The immediate application is AI-assisted radiology reporting. A generated report can be parsed into clinical
statements, and the proposed validator can identify which statements are image-supported, uncertain, contradicted,
or possibly missing. The radiologist remains responsible for the final report, but receives a structured safety signal.
A second application is pre-deployment auditing of generative radiology systems. The framework can be used to
measure statement-level coverage, abstention, set size, rare-finding behavior, uncertainty mismatch, contradiction
rates, and omission patterns before clinical use.
A third application is post-deployment monitoring. Changes in calibrated set size, abstention rate, or subgroup
performance may indicate reduced reliability and motivate review or recalibration.
Although the empirical focus is radiology, the framework is applicable to other medical domains in which
generated text makes data-grounded clinical statements.
